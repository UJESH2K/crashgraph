## 0:22 — 0:52 · What it is, and what the policy actually is

**Screen:** bring the panels back with `]`. Flight running. Then the **Memory** stage — the 3D
graph — and orbit it once.

> Reinforcement learning normally ends with a neural network: a block of numbers that lands the
> craft and can tell you nothing about it. When it fails you retrain. You can't ask it why,
> because there's nothing in there to ask.
>
> crashgraph removes the network. Before every engine decision the pilot reads FalkorDB. After
> every attempt it writes back what happened. The policy **is** the graph. That's the whole
> design: an agent whose decision loop is a database round-trip, fifty times a second.

---

## 0:52 — 1:30 · The data, and why the graph model earns its place

**Screen:** the Memory stage. Hover a node so the readout fills. Then the growth chart at the
bottom, then the **"the two queries this view is built from"** disclosure.

> Every number here is generated by the thing itself. The physics is Gymnasium's LunarLander on
> Box2D, unmodified — standard reward, standard dynamics — so the scores compare directly with
> any other result on the same problem. There's no borrowed dataset. It flies, it writes down
> what happened, and that's the data.
>
> And it goes in as three kinds of memory in one graph. **Procedural** — a hundred and eight
> thousand state tiles holding the value of each engine action. **Semantic** — seventeen hundred
> situations and eight thousand transitions between them, which is what crash risk is computed
> from. **Episodic** — every attempt it has ever flown, and the exact path it took.
>
> A weight matrix can hold the first one. It cannot hold the other two, and it certainly can't
> let you query across them.

## 1:30 — 2:05 · The proof

**Screen:** Panel → **Proof** tab → run the removal test. Let the three bars fill.

> So is the graph actually load-bearing, or is it a nice picture next to a model that's doing
> the real work? That's the test, and it runs from the UI.
>
> Thirty seeded flights with the graph: twenty-six landings. Wipe it, fly the identical seeds:
> **zero.** Restore from a `GRAPH.COPY` snapshot, same seeds again: twenty-six, and the scores
> come back identical to the digit — because the same seeds against the same graph produce the
> same flight.
>
> There is nowhere else for the skill to be hiding.

---

## 2:05 — 2:40 · What a queryable policy buys you

**Screen:** Panel → **Why** tab on a crashed attempt, scroll the explanation, open the Cypher
disclosure. Then the world picker — Luna → Ares — and the **Transfer skills** control.

> Once the policy is data, things that are impossible with weights become ordinary queries.
>
> Ask why an attempt crashed and you get the point of no return, the decision one step earlier
> that mattered, and what the attempts that survived did instead — each with the Cypher printed
> next to it. PageRank over the transitions finds the states that funnel attempts into crashes.
> Label propagation groups them into failure families.
>
> It's multi-tenant: one FalkorDB instance, one isolated graph per world. Luna, Ares, Titan —
> different gravity, different wind. And because procedural memory is just nodes, you can copy
> one agent's skills into another and watch it start competent in a world it has never seen.
>
> Snapshot, restore, transfer, inspect. That's a memory substrate, not a lander trick.

## Notes for the recording

**The one thing to get right.** The cold open is the pitch. If the wipe-and-restore reads
clearly in the first twenty seconds, the rest is just explanation. Rehearse that cut until it
is tight and do not narrate over it.

**Numbers, verified at the time of writing.** Luna: 3,743 attempts, 64% landing rate overall,
108,061 tiles, 1,744 situations, 8,775 transitions. Removal test on the trained Luna graph:
26/30 → 0/30 → 26/30. Ares 1,572 attempts, Titan 1,533, both seeded from Luna's tiles. Re-read
these off the screen before you record — the trainer keeps running and they will have moved.

**Say "synthetic" if anyone asks about data.** It is the correct answer and it is a strength:
every figure in the video can be reproduced from a clean clone, and the removal test is the
reason anyone should believe the rest.

**What to leave out.** The tile-coding and Sarsa(λ) detail, the Reactor render layer, the MCP
server, the writer lease, the three-stage UI tour. All real, none of it is the argument. If
there is a Q&A, the MCP server is the best thing to bring up second — it means any LLM agent
can query this memory, which is the point of the "context for AI agents" theme.
